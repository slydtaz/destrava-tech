Artificial intelligence can be the best private tutor you have had — or the crutch that stops you from learning. The difference is **how** you use it. This guide shows a practical method for AI to speed up your studies without cheating or memorizing wrong answers.

## The rule

> **Use AI to train your brain, not to replace it.**

If AI does the work and you hand it in, the AI learned. If AI explains, asks questions, and corrects you while **you** think, you learn. This is practical: the test, the vestibular, and life do not come with a chatbot beside you.

## The method in 5 steps

### 1. Ask for an explanation, not the answer

Instead of "solve this equation", ask:

> "Explain step by step how to solve quadratic equations, as if I were 13 years old. Then give me a similar example to try on my own."

AI explains the path; you practise the route.

### 2. Use the explain-it-back technique (Feynman)

After studying a topic, write to the AI:

> "I will explain what I understood about photosynthesis. Point out what is wrong, incomplete, or confusing in my explanation."

Explaining in your own words is the most honest test of learning — and AI is an infinitely patient listener.

### 3. Generate practice questions

> "Create 10 multiple-choice questions about the French Revolution, high school level, with an answer key and explanations at the end."

Do the questions **without looking at the answer key**. Then check and ask for explanations for the ones you got wrong. Mistakes here are free; mistakes on the test are not.

### 4. Simulate tests and interviews

> "Give me a 5-question oral test on this subject, one question at a time, and evaluate my answers."

Or, for languages: "Talk to me in basic English and correct my mistakes after each answer."

### 5. Ask for realistic plans

> "I have 45 minutes a day and a math test in 3 weeks. Make a study plan with spaced review, starting from zero."

AI plans are a good draft — adjust them to your reality and follow them.

## The three dangers (and how to handle them)

### Danger 1: AI invents with confidence

AI models sometimes produce false information with complete confidence: wrong dates, swapped formulas, invented citations. **Rule:** check an important fact (date, formula, law, exam concept) in your textbook, your teacher's material, or an official source. AI is a starting point, not the final source.

### Danger 2: the illusion of understanding

Reading a perfect explanation gives a feeling of mastery — but recognizing is not knowing. **Rule:** close the chat and redo it alone, on paper, without consulting anything. If you get stuck, you have not learned it yet; go back to step 1.

### Danger 3: cheating is fraud (and costs you)

Handing in AI work as your own is academic dishonesty — and many schools and universities already treat it as cheating, with real penalties. On top of that, you pay the highest price: you reach the test without knowing the material. **Rule:** use AI to study; write your work with your own head. If your school allows AI at some stage, ask your teacher and declare its use.

## Ready-to-use prompt kit (copy and adapt)

- "Explain [topic] at three levels: a 10-year-old, high school, and detailed."
- "What are the 5 most common mistakes people make when learning [topic]?"
- "Give me an everyday analogy for [concept]."
- "Create a question-and-answer flashcard for each concept in this list: [...]"
- "Correct my essay by pointing out structure, argumentation, and grammar, without rewriting it for me."

Note the last one: "without rewriting it for me".

## Summary

AI is a trainer, not a stand-in. Ask for explanations, explain it back, practise with generated questions, check important facts in official material, and write your own work.
